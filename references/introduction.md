# Introduction

Source: distilled from Unit 1 of the book and its opening chapter.

The Introduction is the wide end of the article's hourglass: it starts general and narrows to the study, then hands the reader to the Methods. Treat the model as a menu, taking what the readers and the target journal need. Plan it paragraph by paragraph before writing sentences, and give each paragraph one function. When revising, keep the author's content and map each paragraph to a move; a missing move becomes a question or a placeholder, never invented content.

## 1. Move model
Four components, usually in this order. Begin with something from component 1 and end with something from component 4.
1. **Orient the reader: importance, background, general problem area.** Gives all readers a shared meeting place. Required as a whole; start with some version of it, and open with the field, not your own specific problem or method. Mix these sub-moves, general first:
   - 1a. Why the topic or field matters. Optional; the usual opener when the target journal's articles open this way, and the default for a wide or interdisciplinary readership.
   - 1b. Accepted background facts, definitions or key terms. Optional; such facts can recur anywhere readers need them. Add citations as the field's current usage requires.
   - 1c. The general problem area or the field's current research focus, extended by a related aspect if needed. Required in function, though it can fold into 1a or 1b. Describe the field's problem, not yet your own.
2. **Previous and/or current research and contributions (the research map).** Shows how the field developed, locates your study on that map and justifies your approach. Required in most articles. It is a narrated, selective path toward your problem, not a list of everything read.
   - Include only studies that are relevant and essential to your motivation; each one leads toward the gap.
   - Use one organizing pattern: general to specific (the most common; often starts from a review article), grouped by approach, theory or model (avoids a back-and-forth of contrasts), or chronological (when the field's development tracks external events or regulation).
   - Some Introductions summarize current knowledge in place of citing individual studies.
   - Depth varies by journal: count how many studies the target articles cite.
3. **Locate the gap and motivate the study.** The problem, gap, unanswered question, hypothesis or opportunity your study responds to. Required in function; the wording may be implicit when the aim is simply to extend earlier work or the gap shows through the description of the study.
   - Signal it with a contrast word so the reader recognizes the turn at once.
   - Shape it as a problem, a gap, a hypothesis or an opportunity. Phrase it as a prediction, suggestion or hypothesis, not as a question.
   - Extra background (properties, a device part) may follow so readers can grasp the gap.
4. **Describe the present paper.** Moves directly to your own work and closes the Introduction. Required. Any mix of:
   - purpose, aim or focus (Optional individually);
   - a brief outline of the method (Optional; keep it short);
   - the main result or conclusion (Optional, common);
   - paper structure or roadmap (Optional; check whether target articles include one);
   - advantages or novelty, with achievement words (Optional; use only what the results support).
   Too much method or result detail makes the Introduction long and unfocused.

Allowed variations: a very narrow field with an expert readership may open with a specific focus, a definition or a fact; several background facts may precede the problem; a paper may state research questions in place of an aim.

### Mirror with the Discussion
The Discussion reverses the Introduction, so keep every Introduction promise answerable there and in the Conclusion.
- **Introduction runs wide to narrow.** Introduction, wide to narrow: field or topic, existing research, the gap, your study.
- **Discussion runs narrow to wide.** Discussion, narrow to wide: your study revisited, your response to the gap, your results mapped onto existing research, your contribution to the field.
- **Lead into the central sections.** The end of the Introduction leads the reader into the central sections; the end of the central sections leads into the Discussion.

## 2. Tense and voice
The general tense rules are in the language reference. For the Introduction:
- **Opening sentence.** First sentence about recent activity: Present Perfect. About the current situation: Present Simple. For a lasting text, prefer a dated anchor to "recently".
- **Established background tense.** Established background facts: Present Simple. A fact starts as a finding (Past Simple), then Present Perfect, then Present Simple with a citation, and finally loses the citation. Check how recent articles treat it now; an outdated tense signals that you are not up to date.
- **Tense of previous research.** Previous research, chosen by meaning:
  - Past Simple ties a finding to that study. It also places a gap in the past, so a Past Simple gap reads as already closed.
  - Present Simple after a reporting verb presents the finding as reliable and established.
  - Present Perfect marks relevance now. It suits a current gap or ongoing research and implies the present paper addresses it.
  - A summary of current knowledge: Present Simple, usually with citations.
- **Tense of the present paper.** The present paper: Present Simple (this paper presents). The aim as a past intention: Past Simple; Present Simple also works, notably when the aim was only partly achieved.
- **Voice in the last move.** Voice in move 4: we/our, the passive, or a non-human subject (this study demonstrates; Section 3 presents). Follow the target journal.
- **Gap wording.** Word the gap impersonally and respectfully about earlier work.

## 3. Phrases by move
- 1a significance: considerable interest in; widely used; has attracted much attention; plays a key role in; a central goal; increasingly popular; an emerging; great potential.
- 1b/1c background and problem area: is produced from; is the most widely used; has been the subject of; a major challenge is.
- 2 linking studies: [ref] were the first to; a pioneering study by; an alternative approach was proposed by; was further developed by; similar work by; subsequently; taken together, these studies suggest; other studies have focused on; to overcome these problems; recently, it has been shown that.
- 2 verbs: achieve, address, assess, demonstrate, develop, identify, investigate, propose, report, show. Choose a specific verb (calculated, monitored, identified) over repeated did/found.
- 3 gap or problem: however; although; little is known about; it is not clear whether; remains unclear / controversial / unstudied; few studies have; has been largely unsuccessful; a drawback / limitation / shortcoming of; lack of; fail to, fall short of, overlook, neglect; time-consuming, impractical; more work is needed.
- 3 opportunity (often with may, might, could): an alternative approach; the next step; an ideal candidate for; an urgent need for.
- 4 present work: in this paper we; this study aims to; the objective of this work was to; to address this issue, we; our approach; this paper is organized as follows; we show that; our results reveal; we propose.
- 4 achievement words (only when the results show them): novel, robust, efficient, effective, simple, accurate, promising, practical, superior, valuable. A first-of-its-kind claim, such as to the best of our knowledge, needs a checked literature behind it.

## 4. Common pitfalls
- **Opening with your own problem.** Opening with your specific problem or method before readers have the background; jumping from very general to very specific, which loses interdisciplinary readers. Err slightly toward too much background.
- **Skipped importance statement.** Skipping the importance statement out of modesty when the target journal's articles make one, or forcing one into a narrow expert-readership paper. Re-check the opening when moving to a journal with a narrower or wider readership.
- **Shopping-list literature review.** A literature review that reads as a shopping list: unordered, irrelevant or unlinked studies, or a back-and-forth of however / on the other hand. Every study must lead to the motivation, and a long review needs pruning.
- **Missing or vague gap.** No gap at all, a gap stated as a question, a gap with no signal word, or a gap the Discussion never answers. In revise mode, build the gap only from what the author's text shows; otherwise ask the author what the earlier work lacks.
- **Achievement words.** Achievement words or first-ever claims (novel, superior, solves, proves) that the results do not support, and promises about all cases when the study covers one. Move the claim down the certainty continuum (language reference) and ask the author for the evidence.
- **Copied tense or citation habit.** Copying an older article's tense or citation habit for a fact that has since become established.
- **Vague this or it.** Vague this or it between gap and aim.
- **Detail in the closing move.** Excess method or result detail in the closing paragraph, or a closing paragraph that never says what the paper does.
- **Hidden familiarity.** Researchers' familiarity with the topic hiding how much the reader needs. Write for a reader, not a colleague.
