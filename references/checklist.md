# Checklist (review mode)

Source: distilled from Unit 8 of the book.

General problems that cut across sections, used when reviewing. Section-specific pitfalls live in each section file; tense, voice, hedging, modals, sentence flow and research verbs live in `references/language.md`. Apply all three, and do not restate a rule from another file; cite it. Format: check, then `"weak" → "better"`.

## How to use it in review
- Read as a first-time reader who is not a specialist. Authors cannot see their own ambiguity. The aim is a text where misunderstanding is impossible, not merely understanding possible.
- Order findings by cost to the reader: (1) anything that changes or hides meaning (ambiguous scope, wrong signal logic, unclear ownership, certainty beyond the evidence); (2) missing or unstated purpose and value; (3) sentence-level readability; (4) tics and look-alike words. Organization and clarity outweigh error-free grammar.
- Name the check in each finding, with a rewrite of the affected sentence only. Put `[placeholder: ...]` where the fix needs a fact, number or citation the author has not given. Write each rewrite in the input's own markup (escaped symbols, `\cite{}` keys, cross-references), so the author can paste it back.
- Everything in the text should lead toward the study's main value. Flag material that does not, and offer to cut it or to say how it serves the point.

## Organizing the information
- **Plan from the destination.** Can the main achievement, and its contribution to knowledge, the field and the real world, be stated in a sentence, and does each paragraph lead there? If not, rank the achievements and rebuild the order. `"A paragraph tracing the lab's instrument history"` → `"A paragraph that traces the instrument history only as far as it explains why a cheaper probe was needed."`
- **Make the value explicit.** Results and data do not speak for themselves; say what they achieve, and keep that message consistent through the paper. Where no impact is obvious, say what the study adds. `"Error fell to 3%."` → `"Error fell to 3%, which makes the probe usable on small farms."`
- **Link a jump from general to technical.** Especially at a subsection start. Add a sentence that connects, using overlap, This + noun, or a signal. `"Fouling limits membranes. We coated polyamide with TiO2."` → `"Fouling limits membranes. To reduce it, we coated polyamide with TiO2."`
- **Give each item a narrative function.** The reader should see whether a sentence states a problem, a solution, or a positive or negative case. `"Sample B had a pH of 4.1."` → `"By contrast, sample B was acidic (pH 4.1)."`
- **Answer "so what?"** at the end of a result or claim. `"The peak shifted by 4 nm."` → `"The peak shifted by 4 nm, which suggests that the film swelled."`
- **State relevance.** Tell the reader why a sentence is there. `"Cells were counted twice."` → `"Cells were counted twice to check counting error."`
- **Supply assumed knowledge.** Think of non-specialists, future readers and readers who skipped earlier sections. `"Gating followed the usual scheme."` → `"Gating, the step that selects cells by size, followed the usual scheme."`
- Paragraph shape and subtitles: see the language reference and the section file.

## Creating sentences
- **Flag key points.** A short opener marks what matters; use it rarely. `"Error doubled at night."` → `"Notably, error doubled at night."`
- **Resolve and/or scope.** If the coordination could group things two ways, rewrite. `"We tested sand and gravel or clay."` → `"We tested either a sand-gravel mix or clay."`

## Grammar and vocabulary
- **Subject-verb agreement.** Check distant subjects, collective nouns and data, and that the subject can do the action. `"The ratio of the two peaks were stable."` → `"The ratio of the two peaks was stable."` `"The graph measured the drift."` → `"We measured the drift; the graph shows it."`
- **Ownership needs a source.** Each statement is your hypothesis, a known truth, or someone's finding. A known truth or someone's finding needs a citation, or a placeholder for one. `"Drift is known to rise with temperature."` → `"Drift is known to rise with temperature [placeholder: citation]."`
- **Terminology currency.** Beyond one term per concept (language reference), check that the term is current in the field. Search your phrase against the common variant in a scholarly index, in quotes and without, and compare counts; repeat before submission. `"leaf-water stress meter"` → the form that dominates recent target articles.
- **Look-alike words.** Check these pairs: alternately / alternatively; beside / besides; criterion / criteria; phenomenon / phenomena; different / various; effective / efficient; e.g. (for example) / i.e. (that is). `"An effective pump uses less power."` → `"An efficient pump uses less power."` The comprise, include, affect, effect, imply, infer and adapt/adopt pairs are in the language reference.
- **Hyphens and respectively.** Hyphenate a compound that modifies a noun; use respectively only when two lists match in order. `"a high temperature process"` → `"a high-temperature process"`. `"Samples A and B weighed 3 and 5 g."` → `"Samples A and B weighed 3 and 5 g, respectively."`

## General
- **Cut lexical tics.** Indeed, in fact, basically, clearly, of course add noise or imply the reader should have known. `"Indeed, the drift was basically linear."` → `"The drift was linear."`
- **Split comma chains.** A run of clauses joined by commas hides the relations. `"We heated it, the peak shifted, the width grew, the area fell."` → `"Heating shifted the peak. The width then grew, and the area fell."`
- **Check certainty against evidence.** Each interpretation and implication must follow from the results and neither inflate nor undersell them. Use the continuum in the language reference to set the rewrite.
- **Check against the target journal.** When unsure, compare with 2-3 recent articles in the target journal (see `references/reverse-engineering.md`).
