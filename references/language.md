# Language reference

Applies to every section and mode. Section-specific tense and voice live in the section file; this file holds the rest. Format: rule, then `"weak" → "better"`. Source: distilled from the book's language notes in Units 1-8 and Appendix B.

## Tense
- **Tense is a choice.** Tense is a choice about function, not only grammar. Past Simple ties a finding to one study. Present Simple presents it as reliable general fact. Present Perfect says it matters now. Switch only on purpose. `"Warming reduces seed set." (one season)` → `"Warming reduced seed set in the 2023 season."`
- **Reporting verb and tense.** Reporting verb plus Past clause reports a finding tied to a study. Reporting verb plus Present clause treats it as settled. `"They found that the filter blocks the dye." (one test)` → `"...blocked the dye."`
- **Past versus Present Perfect.** Past vs Present Perfect differs in link to now, not in time. `"The group has measured the gap in 2019."` → `"The group measured the gap in 2019."` or `"The gap has been measured in several alloys."`
- **Date-stamp current claims.** Date-stamp "current" claims so they stay true. `"No model exists for X."` → `"As of 2024, no model for X has been validated."`
- **Wrong tense changes strength.** A wrong tense can turn a finding into an accepted truth, or a conclusion stronger than the data. Check tense choices against recent target-journal articles.

## Voice and ownership
- **Report your own actions.** To report your own actions use we, the passive, or a non-human subject. Follow the journal. The passive is still normal for procedures.
- **Agentless passives.** Agentless passives hide who acted. Name the agent or add here / in this study. `"It was concluded that drift is linear."` → `"Here we conclude that drift is linear."`
- **Non-human subject.** A non-human subject plus Present Simple reads as someone else's work or background. `"Simulation suggests the design is stable."` → `"Our simulations suggested the design is stable."`
- **Fixed referent of we.** Keep the referent of we fixed. For field-wide statements use it is known / it is now possible. `"We now train models on phone data." (the field)` → `"It is now possible to train models on phone data."`
- **Late main verb.** Do not leave the main verb to the end of a long passive. `"Samples cooled overnight and filtered twice were analyzed."` → `"We analyzed samples that had been cooled overnight and filtered twice."`
- **Citation next to claim.** Put each citation next to the claim it supports. `"A, B and C reported X, Y and Z [1-3]."` → `"A reported X [1]; B reported Y [2]; C reported Z [3]."`

## Certainty continuum
Place your belief between speculation and proof, then choose the language for that point. The evidence sets the point, never the wish to sound strong. Three tools move it and combine: the causal verb, the tense, and risk reducers. It applies wherever you claim something: Abstract, Results, Discussion, Conclusion, Title.
- **Causal verbs.** Causal verb, strong to weak: cause, produce, be due to, give rise to; then lead to, result in, drive, influence; then contribute to, be a factor in; then be associated with, be linked to, accompany. Related/linked says nothing about cause. `"Fertilizer caused the yield rise." (correlational data)` → `"The yield rise was associated with fertilizer use."`
- **Direction of the verb.** Direction is fixed by the verb: X results from Y means Y is the cause; X results in Y means X is the cause. Check it against your data.
- **Articles change strength.** Articles change strength: a cause of = one of several; the cause of = the only one. `"the cause of the delay"` → `"a cause of the delay"` unless you have excluded the rest.
- **Tense moves certainty.** Tense moves certainty without changing data (see Tense). Keep Past Simple when results are preliminary.
- **Risk reducers.** Risk reducers: it appears that; the evidence points to the possibility that; often, in some cases; certain types of; may, might, could. `"Heat damages the film."` → `"Heat often damaged the film in our samples."`
- **Certainty levels.** Levels, most to least certain:
  - near-certain: provides strong evidence for; it is evident that;
  - probable: is highly likely to; is most plausibly explained by;
  - plausible: these results indicate; the data suggest; it appears that;
  - possible: it is possible that; may be explained by; could be interpreted as;
  - speculative: we speculate that; the evidence points to the possibility that; perhaps.
- **One hedge per claim.** Use one hedge per claim; stacked hedges say nothing. `"The data might possibly hint that the drug may work."` → `"The data suggest that the drug lowers blood pressure."`
- **Hedging runs both ways.** Hedging runs both ways: a claim the evidence supports firmly gets a firm verb. `"It may be that the effect is perhaps real." (five replicates agree)` → `"The effect was reproducible across five replicates."`
- **Confirming verbs.** Verbs that confirm, strong to weak: prove, confirm, corroborate, verify, validate, support. Verbs that oppose: disprove, refute, contradict. Soften with appear to / tend to. `"The result proves the model."` → `"The result supports the model."`
- **Value words.** Value words (robust, efficient, novel, first, low-cost) state value only when the paper shows it. `"A novel, robust sensor."` → `"A sensor whose error stayed low across three soils."`
- **Evaluate numbers in words.** Evaluate numbers in words so the reader knows what they mean. `"Accuracy was 43%."` → `"Accuracy was only 43%."` (or `as high as 43%`).
- **Objective and subjective.** Objective is a comparative or a number; subjective is an assessment. Give the objective form, then comment. `"Loss was high."` → `"Loss was 8% higher than at baseline, a striking difference."`
- **Frequency words.** Frequency words need a level the data justify: always, usually, often, sometimes, rarely, never. No frequency word gives the reader nothing to weigh. `"Cracks form."` → `"Cracks formed in most specimens."`
- **Comparison with other work.** Comparison with other work: consistent with, in agreement with, comparable to, unlike, in contrast to, differs from. `"Our rate matches theirs exactly."` → `"Our rate is consistent with theirs."`

## Modal verbs
Use the formal written senses only. Modals place a claim on the continuum. When unsure of a modal, use the alternative structure (it is possible that, is expected to, is able to).
- **Possibility modals.** Possible: may, might, could (might is weaker and rarer). Negative possibility is may not / might not; could not and cannot mean impossible. `"The shift could not be due to noise." (possibly not)` → `"The shift may not be due to noise."`
- **Possibility about the past.** Possibility about the past needs a perfect form; not can have. `"The drop may be caused by drift." (earlier event)` → `"The drop may have been caused by drift."`
- **Ambiguous can.** Can is ambiguous (possible, able, sometimes). `"Defects can occur."` → `"Defects occasionally occur."` or `"The process is able to remove defects."` Use can for options: `"A or B can be used."`
- **Able to.** Able: choose is able to when unsure; the future is will be able to. `"The probe will can resolve it."` → `"The probe will be able to resolve it."`
- **Ambiguous should.** Should has two meanings, expectation and advice. Rephrase when either reading is possible. `"The cells should be washed."` → `"The cells must be washed."` (procedure) or `"The cells are expected to wash clean."` (prediction)
- **Must as inference.** Must (obvious) signals inference without direct evidence. State the evidence instead. `"The sample must have been contaminated."` → `"The blank showed contamination."` or `"The sample was probably contaminated."`
- **Must not versus need not.** Must not = forbidden; need not / do not have to = not necessary. `"Users must not recalibrate." (merely unneeded)` → `"Users need not recalibrate."`
- **Modals where data are strong.** Do not use may/might to play safe where the data are strong, nor must/clearly where they are weak. `"The drug may reduce mortality." (the trial showed it)` → `"The drug reduced mortality."`
- **Projection language.** Projection language (will help, should enable, paves the way for) belongs to applications, not to what the data show.

## Sentence flow
- **Known before new.** Put known information first and new information last; the new information then starts the next sentence. `"A drop was caused by the filter in pressure."` → `"The filter caused a drop in pressure. This drop..."`
- **Link sentences.** Link each sentence to the last by overlap (repeat a key word early), a pro-form with its noun (this method), a semicolon between two close sentences, or a signal word. Use signals sparingly; mix them with overlap.
- **Signal function.** A signal states the sentence's function. Therefore claims a consequence, moreover a same-direction addition, in other words a true restatement. If the link is not obvious, add the missing information instead of the signal. `"Samples were stored cold. Therefore, we used PCR."` → `"Samples were stored cold. We then used PCR."`
- **Signals by function.** Signals by function:
  - cause: because, since, due to; result: therefore, thus, as a result;
  - contrast: however, whereas, in contrast; unexpectedness: although, despite, nevertheless;
  - addition: in addition, furthermore, also; transition: regarding, turning now to.
- **Ambiguous as, since, while.** As, since and while are ambiguous (because/when, from/because, whereas/at the same time). `"Since heated, it cracked."` → `"After heating, it cracked."`
- **Signal position.** Put the signal at the start of a long sentence, not the end. `"...in every sample, however."` → `"However, in every sample..."`
- **Sentence length.** Aim for sentences under 20 words; at over 40 words few readers follow. The average is about 22. Consecutive long sentences are worst. Split at and, which, and piled prepositions.
- **Sentence openings.** Open sentences with a subject or signal, not a bare -ing form or preposition. `"From this estimate, the cost fell."` → `"Using this estimate, we found the cost fell."`
- **Prepositional chains.** A chain of prepositional phrases blurs what modifies what. `"A test of the effect of heat on the strength of the weld"` → `"A test of how heat weakens the weld"`
- **Ambiguous with.** With is the most ambiguous preposition. Use by + -ing for a process and using for a tool. `"Coated with X"` → `"Coated by dipping in X"`
- **Prepositions change meaning.** Prepositions change meaning: evidence of X (X exists) vs evidence for X (X may exist); substituted for Y (replaced Y) vs substituted with Y. Copy verb + preposition pairs from target articles.
- **Noun after this.** Add a noun after this / these / that, and repeat the noun when it/which could point to two things. `"This reduces cost."` → `"This substitution reduces cost."`
- **Placement of only.** Place only, just, simply right before the word they limit. `"We only measured pH."` → `"We measured only pH."`
- **Ambiguous -ing clause.** An -ing clause can mean by, when, because or thereby. State the link. `"Heating the sample, the peak shifted."` → `"Because we heated the sample, the peak shifted."`
- **Articles carry meaning.** Articles carry meaning: a = one of many or new; the = shared or unique; zero = general plural or uncountable. Uncountable nouns (evidence, equipment, research) take no a and no plural. `"an equipment"` → `"a piece of equipment"`
- **Long noun stacks.** Break long noun stacks with a verb or a clause. `"Soil nitrogen loss reduction method"` → `"a method that reduces nitrogen loss from soil"`
- **One name per concept.** One name per concept; do not vary terms for style. `"our method... the framework... the scheme"` → `"our method... the method... the method"`
- **Paragraph entry sentence.** Open each paragraph with an entry sentence naming its function. One function per paragraph; about 150-170 words; no runs of one-sentence paragraphs.
- **Dashes and parentheses.** Replace dash pairs and parentheses that carry real content with words or a new sentence.

## Research verbs
Choose the specific verb, and match its strength to the evidence. `"We got a lower error."` → `"We obtained a lower error."`
- **Presenting verbs.** Presenting: present, propose, describe, report, examine, investigate, address, introduce, outline.
- **Method verbs.** Method: collect, measure, record, calibrate, synthesize, simulate, apply, perform, select, optimize.
- **Analysis verbs.** Analysis: calculate, estimate, quantify, evaluate, determine, characterize, derive.
- **Result verbs.** Results: increase, decrease, reach, exceed, peak, remain, yield, reveal, exhibit, vary, converge, deviate from.
- **Supporting verbs.** Supporting: support, confirm, corroborate, validate, verify, correspond to, accord with.
- **Contradicting verbs.** Contradicting: contradict, conflict with, challenge, refute, rule out.
- **Interpreting verbs.** Interpreting, cautious to strong: appear to, seem to, suggest, indicate, imply, speculate, propose, argue, conclude, demonstrate.
- **Value verbs.** Value: enable, improve, outperform, simplify, overcome, facilitate, shed new light on, provide insight into.
- **Look-alike verb pairs.** Pairs to keep apart: affect (influence) / effect (bring about); imply (suggest) / infer (conclude); adapt (modify) / adopt (choose); comprise (all parts) / include (some parts).
