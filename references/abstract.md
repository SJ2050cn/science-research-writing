# Abstract

Source: distilled from Unit 6 of the book.

The Abstract is read far more than the paper, usually alone, by fast, often non-specialist readers deciding whether to read on. It must work as a standalone text: make misunderstanding impossible, not merely understanding possible. It selects from the finished paper and adds no new content. Re-check it against the final paper and the journal's rules before submission.

## 1. Move model
Not every Abstract has every move. Combine moves to save words. Value words (accurate, robust, practical) can sit in any move.
1. **Significance of the topic / essential background.** Orients non-specialists. One or two sentences at most. Optional; more useful for interdisciplinary readers.
2. **Challenge / problem / gap.** Why the study was needed. Optional but common; often merged with move 3.
3. **What the study does.** Purpose or scope and, where possible, the achievement in the same sentence. Required; often the first or second sentence.
4. **Method / materials.** Only what the reader needs to judge relevance (simulation, model or field data; many experiments or one case). Required, short. Give numbers only for key parameters.
5. **Results.** The key findings only, with numbers where they matter and wording that frames them (only, as high as). Required.
6. **Mapping to existing knowledge.** How the findings fit, extend, confirm or contradict earlier work. Optional.
7. **Achievement / value / contribution.** The single most important thing the study gives the field, signaled explicitly. Required; at least one sentence must make it unmistakable.
8. **Implications.** What the results suggest or mean, at the strength the evidence allows. Optional.
9. **Applications.** Where the work could be used. Optional.

Ordering and variants
- **Flexible order.** Order is flexible. A common run is background, problem, what the study does, method, results, implications. Value or purpose can come early.
- **Abstract types.** Types: a single paragraph (80-250 words typical, about 22 words per sentence); a structured abstract with journal headings (Background/Methods/Results/Conclusions, or Objective/Approach/Main results/Significance), each heading mapping onto the same moves; an Abstract plus a Significance Statement or Highlights; a graphical abstract.
- **Significance statement and highlights.** A Significance Statement is for a broad readership: background, gap, what the study shows, why it matters. Highlights are short parallel bullets of results. Treat both as add-ons, never as substitutes for the Abstract.
- **Graphical abstract.** A graphical abstract is usually one single-panel image giving the take-home message at a glance. Keep it uncluttered and use color sparingly. It does not replace the written Abstract.
- **Limit is a ceiling.** The word limit is a ceiling, not a target. Pick the central achievement and say so, rather than summarizing the whole paper; listing every achievement scatters impact.
- **Combine moves to fit.** To fit a limit, combine moves: purpose + achievement; method + result in one sentence; result + implication (`..., suggesting that...`). Keep the links between sentences.
- **Derive the model from the journal.** For a target journal, derive the model from 2-3 of its Abstracts (see the reverse-engineering reference).

## 2. Tense and voice
- **Tense for what the study does.** What the study does: Present Simple (this paper presents; here we show).
- **Tense for method.** Method: Past Simple.
- **Tense for results and implications.** Results and implications: Past Simple or Present Simple. Present is common in Abstracts for impact, even when the body uses past.
- **Tense for achievement and value.** Achievement and value: Present Perfect or Present Simple.
- **Tense for background facts.** Background facts: Present Simple.
- **Show ownership.** Show ownership. Present Simple results and non-human subjects (`modeling suggests`) read as established fact or as prior work. To claim a result as yours, use Past Simple or Present Perfect, active we, or an opener such as `In this study` / `Here`; use `It is known that` for others' work.
- **Passives for method.** Passives are fine for method. Give your own claims an agent (we, here, in this study).
- **Plain cross-disciplinary vocabulary.** Use plain vocabulary that works across disciplines. Spell terms out; define an essential acronym once.
- **Cite rarely.** Cite rarely: only when the paper directly continues, advances or contradicts a specific published work.
- **Match implications to the data.** Match implications to the data. For early-stage or potential value use may, might, could, possible, potentially, suggest (see the language reference).

## 3. Phrases by move
- Background: is widely accepted that; is known to; is fundamental to; has recently been shown.
- Challenge: little work has so far; until now; remains a difficult problem; is time-consuming, costly, impractical; a need for.
- What the study does: this paper presents; here we show; in this study we investigate; we report; aims to; address, examine, propose, describe, provide.
- Method: were analyzed / measured / recorded / selected; using; rather than; instead of.
- Results: reveal, demonstrate, exhibit, yield, reach, reduce; were observed / achieved / found; only 38% / as high as 38%.
- Mapping: similar to; comparable with; improves on existing; unlike earlier; in contrast to.
- Value: a practical / robust / efficient / low-cost; enables, outperforms, simplifies, solves, validates, improves. Use value words only when the paper shows the value.
- Implications: suggests that; indicates that; appears to; we conclude that; may, might, could; potentially.
- Applications: potential for use as; can be applied to; suitable for; will enable; relevant for.

## 4. Common pitfalls
- **Not standalone.** An Abstract that cannot stand alone: purpose, method, key results or significance missing, or it depends on the paper's text, figures or tables.
- **Contribution unclear.** Contribution unclear: Present Simple results or non-human subjects read as background or other people's work.
- **Padding to the limit.** Padding to the limit with minor method or result details, leaving no clear take-home message.
- **Ambiguous it, this, which.** Ambiguous it / this / which, or different names for the same thing.
- **Dense sentences and jargon.** Long dense sentences, acronym clusters, undefined jargon.
- **Unsupported claims.** Implications or claims the data do not support, and a figure or claim that appears only in the Abstract.
- **Written too early or pasted.** Written too early, or cut and pasted to meet a new journal's limit, leaving broken flow or content absent from the final paper.
- **Stacked citations and missing value.** Citations stacked without need; no value words, so the reader must infer why the study matters.
- **Broken links after compression.** Missing links between sentences after compression; dropping `In this study` / `Here`, which identify your contribution.
- **Draft number missing from notes.** In draft mode, a number the notes lack: never estimate it; write `[placeholder: …]`.
