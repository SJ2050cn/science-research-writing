# Methods

Source: distilled from Unit 2 of the book (Methods), with the Methods items of the checklist unit.

The Methods and Results sections are the narrow middle of the article, between the narrowing Introduction and the widening Discussion. Field labels vary (Materials and methods, Experimental, Simulations, Model, Calibration); use the target journal's label. The section exists so another researcher can repeat the work and get similar results. Write it for a reader who needs to know why and how the method relates to other work, not only the steps. Plan the whole section before writing sentences.

## 1. Move model
A menu, not a fixed sequence. Pick the moves the topic and journal use. Moves 2, 4, 5 and 6 usually interleave and repeat for each procedure.
1. **Overview of the method.** Optional, but advised unless every reader knows this kind of method. A top-down frame (scope of the work, number of tests, materials, equipment, restated aim, source of samples) so the reader sees the wall before the bricks. A very specialized readership may allow opening on the procedure.
2. **Details of materials and methods.** Required. What was done or used, with quantities, conditions, equipment and sequence. Two optional add-ons per step: a justification (reason, advantage, property that made a choice suitable) and a care signal (the work was done precisely, repeatedly, independently). Open each subsection with a general entry sentence, then give details top-down.
3. **Describe a figure or table.** Optional. Use when a setup, geometry, map or parameter set is easier to show than tell. Say what the graphic shows, and make clear when it is your own setup.
4. **Refer to methods in other studies.** Optional, but required wherever the procedure comes from someone else. It compares (identical, similar, significantly different) and justifies. State exactly what changed and why; a significant difference may be part of the study's contribution. For a well-known source, cite it and add a short summary so the reader need not look it up.
5. **Background information.** Optional. Facts about the site, material, instrument or standard procedure that support the reader or justify a choice. Pitch it at the least-informed reader of the journal.
6. **Issues or problems.** Optional; use when something limited the method. Report the difficulty where it occurs, frame it professionally (minimize it, show it was unavoidable, or point to a remedy) and return to it later as future work. Silence about a known weakness suggests the author is unaware of it.

Variations
- **Supplementary material.** Long technical detail may go in supplementary material, but the main text must give enough method for the results to make sense. Cite supplementary items specifically.
- **Check target-journal balance.** Check 2-3 target-journal articles for balance, placement, length and passive versus active (see the reverse-engineering reference).

**Reproducibility checklist**
- **Quantities and conditions.** Quantities, conditions, equipment make and model, source of materials, software and versions, order and timing of steps. Slightly too much detail beats too little.
- **Precise sequence words.** Precise sequence words, not only then and next: how soon or how long between steps, what ran at the same time, what ended the sequence.
- **Reasons for choices.** A reason for each choice and each departure from a cited protocol.
- **Credit borrowed methods.** Credit for every borrowed method, plus the exact modifications.
- **Limits on replication.** Limits that could affect replication (approximate measurements, contamination risk, unavoidable shortcuts).
- **Units and abbreviations.** Units, symbols and abbreviations that match the journal and SI.
- **Draft detail missing from notes.** In draft mode, any quantity, model, version, condition or step the notes lack becomes `[placeholder: what is needed]`. Never estimate it or fill it from typical practice.

## 2. Tense and voice
- **Past for what was done.** Past Simple for what was done and used in this study.
- **Present for standard procedures.** Present Simple for standard procedures, how an established method or instrument works, background facts, and what a figure or table shows.
- **Tense slips change meaning.** A tense slip changes meaning: past reads as "we did this", present reads as "this is the normal procedure".
- **Explicit ownership.** Make ownership explicit where your work, others' work and routine practice could look the same (see the language reference): an active verb, a marker (in this study, here, in our model), or a neutral subject. For others' work add a citation, "in their work" or "as in [ref]".
- **Present-tense method.** When your method is written in the present in your field, mark it as yours.
- **Passive versus active.** Passive versus active: follow the journal's guide and articles, then the author's profile. Some processes are still normally passive in Methods.
- **Tense check after drafting.** After drafting, check every sentence for tense and for who acted.
- **Three kinds of verbs.** Verbs here fall into three kinds: general research verbs (conduct, determine, verify), discipline-specific verbs (leave to the field), and technical verbs shared across fields (calibrated, mounted, normalized).
- **Place first-time claims.** Place "for the first time" precisely when claiming a novel method or application, and only when the paper supports it.

## 3. Phrases by move
Fragments; adapt to the field.
- Overview: involved collecting; to determine; in all cases; a total of; samples were drawn from.
- Source: was obtained from; was supplied by; was a gift from; was used as received; was generated using; was chosen.
- Set-up: positioned on opposite sides; aligned with; mounted on; fitted with; perpendicular to; upstream of; enclosed in; spaced apart. Just, directly and right above differ in closeness, so pick deliberately.
- Sequence:
  - before: beforehand, prior to, previously, in advance;
  - start: initially, to begin with, at the start;
  - order only: then, next, followed by, subsequently;
  - short wait: soon, shortly after; long wait: later, eventually, towards the end;
  - at once or in parallel: while, when, as soon as, at the same time, meanwhile, until, once, simultaneously;
  - end: finally, lastly, afterwards.
- Justification: in order to; so as to; to this end; for simplicity; selected on the basis of; thereby; so that; the advantage of. Advantage verbs: ensure, enable, facilitate, avoid, prevent, minimize, overcome, validate, simplify. Adjectives: accurate, robust, suitable, precise, consistent.
- Care: carefully; gently; thoroughly; separately; independently; randomly; immediately; freshly; repeatedly; at least three times.
- Other study, identical: as described by/in; according to; following; using the method of; in accordance with. Use by or of with a researcher, in with the work.
- Other study, similar: adapted from; based on; a modified version of; essentially the same as; with minor modifications; except that.
- Other study, different: unlike; instead of; loosely based on; with the following modifications; whereas.
- Summary of a cited method: as in [ref]. Briefly, ...
- Figure or table: is shown in Fig. X; Table X lists; is illustrated in.
- Background: is commercially available; is widely used; is well established.
- Problems, minimizing: negligible; minor; only approximate; less than ideal; rather time-consuming.
- Problems, no blame: unavoidable; inevitably; not possible; limited by; impractical.
- Problems, remedy: acceptable; reasonably robust; future work should (invites others) versus future work will (own plans).

## 4. Common pitfalls
- **Pasted lab notes.** Pasting lab notes: they record steps for the writer, with no reasons or context for a reader.
- **Steps without reasons.** Bare steps with no reason, so the reader questions the choice.
- **Detail without framing.** Bottom-up detail with no framing sentence.
- **Assumed knowledge.** Assuming colleagues' knowledge, when interdisciplinary and search-engine readers will arrive.
- **Only then and next.** Only then and next, losing the timing and simultaneity needed to replicate.
- **Citation without description.** Citing a method and describing nothing, or calling a method "the same" while omitting a change.
- **Misplaced credit.** A citation placed so it credits others with your step, or you with theirs.
- **Agentless passives.** Agentless passives that blur whose work it is; a tense that shifts the meaning.
- **Hidden problems.** Hiding or omitting known problems, or mentioning limits first at the end of the article; using strong negatives where minimizing language is conventional.
- **Detail only in supplement.** Vital method detail only in supplementary files, so the results cannot be understood.
- **Vague amounts.** Vague amounts or conditions (some, high temperature) in place of values and units.
- **Inconsistent units.** Abbreviations and units inconsistent with the journal's convention.
- **Target articles unchecked.** Not checking target articles for length, placement and voice.
- **Invented detail.** Inventing a missing detail to make the method look complete.
