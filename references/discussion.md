# Discussion

Source: distilled from Unit 4 of the book.

The Discussion widens what the Introduction narrowed: your study, the response to the gap, your study mapped onto existing research, then the contribution to the field and the world. The opening echoes the Introduction's last block in reverse; the end is an exit back to the research community and the real world. Readers skim, so the take-home message must not be buried.

Before drafting or reviewing, fix the study's single main value: a better method, better results, a change of direction, or new applications. It sets the destination and the first sentence. Every other move serves it. Results say what was found; the Discussion says what it means.

## 1. Move model
Order is fluid: higher moves tend to come early, lower ones late. Moves 3, 4 and 5 are alternative ways to reboot the reader; most Discussions use at least one.
1. **Announce the structure.** A short orienting sentence. Optional and uncommon; most sections skip it.
2. **State the achievement and contribution.** Required, and the most free-flowing move. The achievement is what the study did against its stated aim (internal). The contribution is how it affects knowledge, the research field and the real world (outward). It may open the section, close it, both, or recur. Reuse the verb the Introduction's aim used.
3. **Revisit the background or literature.** Optional. Reorients readers who skipped or forgot earlier sections; echo the earlier wording so they can find the source.
4. **Revisit the gap, aim or method.** Optional. Choose by the main value: method value revisits the old method's difficulties; results value revisits the results; gap value says to what extent the gap closed.
5. **Revisit the results and explore their implications.** Required. Restate a key result only to anchor an interpretation, explanation or implication, then move on. Say how it answers the original question or hypothesis.
6. **Map to existing knowledge.** Required. Say whether the study confirms, extends, contradicts or improves prior work. Every citation needs its relevance made explicit; the sentence opener carries the relationship. New citations are fine here if they supply interpretation or applications.
7. **Address unexpected or discrepant results.** Optional, but expected when results conflict with the hypothesis or the literature. Flag the surprise, give evidence-based possible explanations, and say what would test them. Keep it inside moves 5 and 6.
8. **State limitations and future work.** Required in the generic model; the amount varies by journal. Say what the study could not do, then turn it into a defined next step. Most limitations first appear in Methods or Results; one may first appear here if it never entered the formal data or if it blocks a firm conclusion. A specific direction gets taken up; a vague one does not.
9. **Restate the achievement, contribution or impact.** Optional. Re-anchors the take-home message near the end.
10. **Identify potential applications.** Optional. May open the section, or be its theme, when applications are the main value. If none are obvious, check how target articles close; some work only clarifies theory.

Sometimes background facts in the Present Simple, with citations, appear late to support the final conclusions or recommendations. A strong closing sentence on applications or impact is common.

Adapting to a journal
- **Label sentence functions.** Label each sentence's function in 2-3 target Discussions, merge with this list, and record: the first sentence's function, deviations from the model, how much of the results is revisited, citation count and placement, and where limitations sit.

## 2. Tense and voice
- **Tense signals confidence.** Tense signals confidence (see the language reference). Judge from the data whether they are robust enough for Present Simple; copying target articles cannot settle it.
- **Key finding phrasing.** A phrase like "a key finding was / is / appears" changes strength with the tense. Check that the tense matches the power of the result.
- **Present for literature facts.** Present Simple for background facts from the literature, including is known to.
- **Reuse earlier wording.** When revisiting the aim, gap or method, reuse the earlier wording and tense. Keep the same words; a changed word makes the reader ask whether it is the same thing.
- **Modals carry speculation.** Modals carry most speculation, applications and recommendations (see the language reference).
- **Name the owner.** Name the owner of each statement: your hypothesis, a known truth, or someone else's finding. Name the agent for your own claims (here, in this study, we).
- **Hedge novelty claims.** Novelty claims need hedging (to our knowledge) and a literature search just before submission, repeated after review, since similar work may have appeared. If it has, state the similarities and differences instead of dropping the contribution.
- **Mark your own plans.** Plans of your own are marked differently from invitations to others.

## 3. Phrases by move
- Contribution and achievement: a first step towards; provides a framework for; to the best of our knowledge; succeeded in; enables; facilitates; robust, reliable, practical, accurate. Strong words (striking, remarkable, unprecedented) only for exceptional evidence.
- Reboot and revisit: this study set out to; as stated earlier; the aim verb repeated; a key finding; particularly.
- Interpretation and implications: it is conceivable that; it is reasonable to assume that; this points to; this is substantiated by; we cannot rule out; we hypothesize that; a tentative explanation is; plausible; suggesting that; which means that.
- Mapping to literature: consistent with; in agreement with; in line with; comparable to; similar to; in contrast to; contrary to; unlike; in conflict with; previously reported. Verbs: confirm, corroborate, support, complement, extend, challenge, contradict, refute, differ from, shed new light on, pave the way for.
- Unexpected results: contrary to; in conflict with; a tentative explanation is; remains unclear; we cannot rule out.
- Limitations: within the limitations of this study; was not addressed formally; further work is needed to.
- Future work for others: future studies should; it would be of interest to; worth investigating; research opportunities include; should be explored / replicated / validated; a starting point; holds promise.
- Own work in progress: work is currently underway; further work is planned; is being investigated.
- Applications: has applications in; could be used to; is applicable to; feasible; viable; suitable; to implement; to generalize; to serve as.
- Citing openers: in line with this; consistent with the literature; in contrast, X reported; to verify this assumption; likewise.

## 4. Common pitfalls
- **No main contribution.** No clear main contribution: the section wanders and the take-home message sinks into detail.
- **Results without interpretation.** Results repeated without interpretation. The reverse error, never naming the key results, leaves interpretations without evidence.
- **Overstatement.** Overstatement (clear, proves, must, in all, will solve) beyond what the results and literature support; a cause claimed from correlational data; advice stated as a result. Reviewers criticize the mismatch.
- **Overhedging.** Overhedging to be safe, or several hedges on one claim, which undersells a firm result. Move up the continuum when the evidence is strong.
- **So what left open.** The "so what?" left open after a result: add suggesting that or which means that, so the reader gets the implication.
- **Vague achievement and impact.** Achievement, contribution and impact stated vaguely or inconsistently (knowledge, research, real world). Make each explicit, with value words backed by the paper, and keep the message the same across Abstract, Introduction, Discussion and Conclusion.
- **Citations without relevance.** Citations whose relevance is not stated, stacked together, or too few to map the study; the comparison with prior work skipped, so the contribution never separates from existing knowledge.
- **Unchecked novelty claims.** Novelty claims (first, largest, novel) with no recent literature check or no hedge.
- **Unexplained contradictions.** Contradicting results mentioned but not explained, or surprises passed over.
- **Hidden limitations.** Limitations or weaknesses hidden, or named with no way forward. Future work vague, or own plans mixed with invitations to others.
- **Changed wording on revisit.** Changed wording or a different verb when revisiting the aim, gap or method.
- **Misused signal words.** Signal words (therefore, thus, in other words) used where the logic is not real; tense switched without reason.
- **Forgotten applications.** Applications or impact forgotten, or added vaguely when they are the main value.
- **No widening at the end.** No widening at the end: the section stops on a detail instead of returning to the field, applications and future work.
- **Mismatched subtitles and keywords.** Subtitles that do not match the content; keywords from the subtitle absent from the subsection's opening.
